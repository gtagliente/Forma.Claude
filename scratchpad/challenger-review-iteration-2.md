# Challenger Review — Iteration 2

_Reviews: `../docs/product/requirements-and-open-items.md` → "Cross-context reference integrity" (Analyst), `../docs/architecture/integration-patterns.md` + `../docs/architecture/adr/ADR-006-cross-service-reference-integrity.md` (Architect), and the corresponding `training-planning-service`/`exercise-service` `open-questions.md` updates. Continuity check against `challenger-review-iteration-1.md`: none of that review's items touch this iteration's topic directly; no regression to flag._

## Verdicts (headline)

- **Point-to-point vs. orchestrator: agree with the Architect.** One bidirectional edge-pair does not justify a third deployable that owns no domain data. Endorsed, with one addition: the deferral needs an explicit trip-wire (see below), not just "revisit if it densifies."
- **Fail-closed on Exercise-delete: not rejected outright, but under-justified as a blanket, ownership-blind policy.** The ADR translates a business rule ("don't allow deleting an in-use Exercise") into a stronger technical guarantee ("don't allow deleting *anything* whenever we can't prove non-use, even for Exercises where the deleting user bears 100% of the risk themselves") without weighing the availability cost against the actual, already-accepted tolerance for dangling references elsewhere in the same design. See finding 1.
- **Top concrete findings**: (1) fail-closed should very likely be scoped to shared Exercises only, not applied uniformly to private ones; (2) fail-open is silent by construction and has no observability requirement, which is a real risk in exactly the environments (dev/CI) where it will fire constantly; (3) the Analyst's User↔everything "near-certain next occurrence" analogy is weaker than presented — existence-checking a User at create time is arguably not the same shape as Exercise↔Workout at all, because authentication already does that job.

## 1. Fail-closed: stress-testing the actual trade-off

**The claim to test**: "Exercise deletion becomes unavailable whenever `training-planning-service` can't confirm no Workout references the Exercise, including when `training-planning-service` itself is down." ADR-006 flags this itself and asks for scrutiny. Taking that seriously:

**The inconsistency**: The Analyst's rule 1 (Workout→Exercise direction) explicitly declares a Dangling Reference an *acceptable, recoverable* outcome — that's the entire justification for building the Dangling-Reference concept and its graceful-degradation UX requirement in the first place ("cheap and immediate to recover from," `requirements-and-open-items.md` rule 1). Rule 2's fail-closed policy is justified by treating the *same end state* — a Workout left holding an ExerciseId that no longer resolves — as categorically unacceptable when it results from an inconclusive delete-check, to the point of taking down an entire feature for every user rather than risk it. But the resulting Dangling Reference looks identical in both cases; only the trigger differs (race condition vs. inconclusive check). The ADR does not explain why the *same outcome* deserves a wholly different tolerance depending on which side produced it — it asserts the asymmetry (false negative vs. false positive) but doesn't weigh it against the magnitude of the cost being paid to avoid it.

**The magnitude problem**: fail-closed blocks *100% of delete attempts* — including deletes of Exercises with zero actual references — for the entire duration of any `training-planning-service` degradation, to prevent a compound, comparatively rare event (check unreachable **and** the specific Exercise being deleted actually has a live reference **and** that reference isn't later fixed). That is a broad, blunt instrument for a narrow risk, and the ADR doesn't attempt this comparison — it jumps from "false negative is the bad outcome" straight to "block everything when uncertain," without considering intermediate options.

**A concrete middle ground the ADR never considers**: surface the uncertainty to the user instead of encoding a blanket system policy. When the check is inconclusive, exercise-service could still block automatically for *shared* Exercises (protects other users who have no agency to resolve the block themselves — this is exactly the asymmetry the Analyst already flagged and the Architect never applied to the mechanism layer), but for a *private* Exercise, allow the delete to proceed with an explicit warning ("we couldn't confirm this isn't used in one of your Workouts — deleting anyway may leave a broken reference you'll need to fix"). The deleting user is, by construction, the only person who can ever be affected by a private Exercise's references (rule 2's own text: "a private Exercise's referencing Workouts always belong to the deleting user"), so blocking them from their own data during a training-planning-service outage is paternalistic, not protective — there is no third party being defended. This would cut the blast radius of the availability trade-off roughly in proportion to how much of the Exercise library ends up private vs. shared (likely the majority, especially early on, per the ownership model in `domain-model.md`), while keeping the strict fail-closed guarantee exactly where the Analyst's own asymmetry argument actually applies — shared Exercises referenced by strangers.

This is not a business-rule change (the Analyst's "block while referenced" rule stands for both) — it is a mechanism-layer refinement (what happens when the mechanism *can't answer* the block question), which is squarely the Architect's remit and doesn't reopen anything the Analyst decided. Recommend the next Architect pass adopt this split, or explicitly justify why not.

**A related, currently-undocumented alternative**: ADR-006's "Alternatives considered" section compares orchestrator vs. mesh vs. async-cache vs. one-uniform-mechanism — but never records "fail-open with mandatory audit logging for reconciliation" as a considered-and-rejected option for rule 2 specifically. Given how directly ADR-006 itself invites Challenger scrutiny on this exact point, its own alternatives list should show the fail-open variant was weighed, not just the four options that were never really in contention for rule 2.

## 2. Fail-open's silence is a real operational risk, not just a residual "narrow race window"

Fail-open is safe only if it fires rarely. Two concrete environments make that assumption shaky:

- **Dev/local/CI for `Forma.Planner`**: `exercise-service` will very often simply not be running when `training-planning-service` is being developed or tested in isolation (they're separate repos/pipelines with no stated docker-compose or contract-test harness). Under fail-open, this means the existence check **silently never validates anything** in exactly the environments where developers most need to see it work — every Workout-create test would pass through the "check inconclusive → proceed" path by default, indistinguishable from "the check ran and confirmed existence." A regression in the check's own plumbing (wrong URL, broken auth, serialization bug) would be masked identically to a legitimate absence of `exercise-service`, and nothing in the design would ever surface that difference.
- **Production**: fail-open produces no user-visible symptom by design — that's the point of the policy — but that also means a *persistent* misconfiguration (not a transient outage) degrading into permanent "always inconclusive" would accumulate dangling references indefinitely without anyone noticing, defeating rule 1's stated goal of "catching the common case early."

Neither `integration-patterns.md` nor ADR-006 mentions logging/metrics for inconclusive-check outcomes. Recommend: any fail-open (and fail-closed) outcome from either check must be logged/counted, with the *rate* of inconclusive results treated as an operational signal (a persistently high rate means the check is broken, not that the network is having a bad day). This is cheap now (a log line / counter) and expensive to retrofit once the check has been silently ineffective for months.

A related, more mundane gap: neither document says how either check is mocked/stubbed so `exercise-service` and `training-planning-service` can each be developed and tested in isolation without standing up the other service. For rule 2 specifically this is more than a dev-experience nit — if the *only* way to locally exercise the "delete succeeds" path is to either run both services together or hand-roll a test double, that should be stated as a required piece of each service's test setup, not left implicit. Filed as an open question.

## 3. Is point-to-point actually going to hold up as edges accumulate?

The Architect's graph analysis (exactly one bidirectional pair, one intra-service instance, one already-solved instance, one not-yet-real instance) is honest and not cherry-picked — I checked it against `requirements-and-open-items.md`'s own "general shape" section and it accurately reflects what's there. Building an orchestrator for one edge-pair would be over-engineering by `CLAUDE.md`'s own test ("every architectural decision must be justified by a real requirement"). Agreed: reject the orchestrator now.

Two things worth tightening rather than reversing:

1. **No trip-wire for revisiting.** "Revisit if the graph genuinely densifies" is exactly the kind of open-ended deferral that risks never actually being revisited — nobody owns checking it, and there's no defined threshold. This costs nothing to fix now: state a concrete trigger, e.g. "revisit the orchestrator question when a *third* service needs to query either `exercise-service` or `training-planning-service` for an existence/reference check, or when two independent edges need materially the same check logic." Writing the trigger down now is cheap; discovering three years from now that "revisit when it densifies" was never operationalized is not.
2. **Formalize the per-edge contract as a living registry, not prose inside one ADR.** The Architect already does the right thing conceptually (narrow single-purpose endpoints, explicit failure-mode per edge) — but this iteration's decision is recorded as prose inside ADR-006/`integration-patterns.md`, tied to these two specific drivers. When the User/`OwnerId` edge (or Routine/Workout, if it ever does cross a service boundary) shows up, whoever designs it will have to re-read this whole ADR to extract the reusable pattern. Recommend `integration-patterns.md` grow a short, explicitly-maintained table ("edge, caller→callee, business rule, mechanism, failure mode on inconclusive") that every future cross-service check gets a row in — cheap now, and it's the concrete artifact that actually prevents the "uncontrolled growing mesh" the product owner was worried about in the abstract, without needing an orchestrator to enforce it.

## 4. The suggested dependency inversion for rule 2 — already substantially true, and the async variant already considered

Worth checking explicitly since it was flagged as a stress-test target: does `exercise-service` asking `training-planning-service` "is this ID referenced" indicate the wrong service is enforcing this? No — the call direction is already correct. `training-planning-service` is the only service that actually holds the answer (it owns every `WorkoutExerciseEntry.ExerciseId`); routing the query to the party that owns the data, rather than exercise-service trying to maintain its own shadow copy of "who references me," is the right shape. There's no inversion available here that doesn't just relocate the same problem.

The *variant* worth naming explicitly — `exercise-service` maintaining a local, asynchronously-updated cache of "currently-referenced Exercise IDs," fed by events from `training-planning-service` — is real, but it's exactly Option (c) in `integration-patterns.md`, already considered and deferred (correctly, for now) mainly because neither codebase has messaging infrastructure yet. One nuance in how that rejection is *justified*, not its conclusion, is worth tightening for next time: the doc frames async caching as categorically "the wrong tool... cannot tolerate" staleness for rule 2, but the actual staleness window such a cache would have (the delay between a Workout referencing an Exercise and that event being consumed) is bounded and arguably comparable in size to the race window rule 1 already accepts as tolerable — it isn't unboundedly stale. The decisive reason to defer async today is the missing infrastructure and the single-consumer economics, which is a suffient reason on its own — the "wrong tool, full stop" framing overstates the case and could mislead a future iteration into thinking async is disqualified in principle rather than just not yet justified. Minor documentation-precision note, not a decision change.

## 5. Challenging the Analyst: is "User↔everything" really the same shape?

`requirements-and-open-items.md` names `OwnerId`/User as "arguably the most certain next occurrence of this shape," and the Architect accepts that framing while correctly declining to design for it yet. Worth pushing back on the framing itself, not just the timing: Exercise↔Workout's existence check exists because a Workout *references an ID the user doesn't otherwise have any independent proof of* at save time. A User reference is different in one structural way that matters — in virtually every real system, the caller creating a Workout/Exercise/Routine is already *authenticated as* the User they're about to set as `OwnerId`. The token/session that authorized the request already proves the User exists (that's what authentication *is*); a live "does this User exist" existence check at create time would be redundant with auth, not a new instance of the same problem. The genuinely analogous case is **account deletion** (does deleting a User need to check/block on references the same way Exercise deletion does?) — a real question, but a low-frequency, cascading one, structurally closer to a one-time fan-out job than to a per-request synchronous check repeated on every single create across every service. Conflating "per-create existence check" with "per-delete reference check" under one "OwnerId fan-out" heading risks the next iteration designing the wrong thing (a high-volume live check) for a problem that's actually low-volume and event-shaped. Recommend the next Analyst/Architect pass split this explicitly into two separate questions once `identity-service` is real, rather than carrying it forward as one bundled "near-certain next occurrence."

## 6. The Analyst's own flagged tension deserves more than "flagged, not resolved"

`requirements-and-open-items.md` rule 2 already surfaces that identical shared/private treatment could make a popular shared Exercise practically undeletable, and files it as open question 3, cross-referenced to the still-open library-governance question (Users, item 3). Both are filed as *separate* deferred items in their respective docs today (`requirements-and-open-items.md` open question 3, `exercise-service/open-questions.md` item 1). They are tightly coupled — there is no point deciding "who can delete a shared Exercise" without also deciding "what happens when they try and it's still referenced," and vice versa — but nothing currently says they should be picked up together. Left as-is, a future iteration could resolve library governance while treating the undeletable-shared-Exercise tension as someone else's problem, or vice versa, and the two decisions could end up incompatible. Recommend bundling them explicitly for whichever iteration picks either one up. (Filed as an open question below rather than a docs edit, per role boundaries.)

## What holds up well

- The Architect's graph analysis (exactly one real bidirectional edge-pair today) is accurate, not inflated or minimized, and the orchestrator rejection built on it is sound.
- Deriving an *asymmetric* mechanism per business rule (rather than one uniform sync/async policy for both directions) is the right level of granularity — better than either "always sync" or "always async" would have been.
- The "already-settled carve-out" callout (Workout Session's denormalized copy is exempt from both concerns) is correctly carried forward and not accidentally reopened.
- No caching/rate-limiting for the per-save check being deferred is fine — no evidence of a scale problem, consistent with "avoid premature complexity."
- The provisional "current-version-only" default for what counts as "referenced" (rule 2) is reasonable and low-risk as a default, and is correctly flagged as unresolved rather than quietly adopted.

## Simplifications proposed for next iteration

1. Split rule 2's failure-mode policy by ownership: fail-closed for shared Exercises, fail-open-with-warning (or at minimum a softer confirmation flow) for private ones — cuts the availability blast radius without weakening the protection where it actually matters (protecting other users' Workouts).
2. Require observability (log/metric) on inconclusive-check outcomes for both rules before either ships — cheap now, otherwise fail-open's correctness is unverifiable in practice.
3. Add a concrete trip-wire condition for revisiting the orchestrator rejection, and turn the per-edge contract table in `integration-patterns.md` into a maintained registry rather than one-off prose.
4. Split the "OwnerId fan-out" question in two (per-create existence vs. account-deletion reference-check) before `identity-service`'s central-loop pass starts, so that pass isn't anchored to a single bundled framing that conflates a probably-unnecessary check with a real one.

## Addendum review — Client-generation tooling for the two ADR-006 endpoint clients (2026-07-12)

_Reviews: `../docs/architecture/integration-patterns.md` → "Client-generation tooling for the two ADR-006 endpoint clients" (marked **Proposed**, per the file's 2026-07-12 addendum note), and the corresponding pointer in `../docs/architecture/adr/README.md`. This is a follow-on decision within Iteration 2 (triggered by ADR-006's own deferred "transport/technology choice" bullet), not a new iteration — appended here rather than filed separately. Continuity check: none of this iteration's earlier findings (above) touch client-generation tooling directly; no regression to flag, but finding 5 above (User/`OwnerId` fan-out framing) turns out to be directly load-bearing for one of this addendum's own arguments — see §4 below._

### Verdicts (headline)

- **Refit over Kiota: agree, for now** — but the sharper risk isn't "wrong generator," it's that Refit+Refitter's manual regeneration has **no drift-detection mechanism at all** (unlike Kiota's `kiota-lock.json`), which is a bigger and more concrete gap than the tool-identity question the document spends most of its comparison table on. See §1.
- **Not a central mandate: agree with the conclusion, but the supporting analogy is weaker than the document thinks.** The precedents cited (`DayOfWeek?`, hierarchy cross-visibility) are single-service, single-author provisional defaults; this is a two-service, same-team, *bidirectionally-coupled* tooling choice — a different shape of "local" decision that deserved to be named as different, not folded into the same precedent bucket. See §2.
- **ADR vs. addendum: the practical call is reasonable, but it's not actually licensed by the ADR README's own written criteria**, which say "affects more than one service/feature" is sufficient *on its own* (an "or", not an "and") to warrant an ADR. This decision affects two services by construction. The document's self-justification for staying out of `adr/` is a *new, unwritten* exception ("non-binding conclusions don't need a binding-format record") that nothing in `adr/README.md` currently sanctions. See §3.
- **Security-gap framing: the flag itself is valuable and, per a repo-wide check, is the first place in this entire project's documentation that explicitly states the system currently has zero authentication anywhere** — not previously written down even in `identity-service/README.md`, which just says "placeholder only." But it's framed at the wrong scope: worded as if it's a property of "these two ADR-006 endpoints," when it's actually a property of *every* endpoint in the system today. That scoping risks the opposite of the intended effect. See §5.

### 1. Refit vs. Kiota — the real gap is regen-drift, not generator identity

The document's four-column comparison table treats "regeneration workflow" as one dimension among several, and picks Refit mainly on ports-and-adapters fit (Refitter's output is literally an interface). That reasoning is sound on its own terms. But look specifically at what "regeneration workflow" means for each:

- **Kiota**: "CLI regen, with built-in lock-file (`kiota-lock.json`) tracking for incremental regen." A lock file gives a *mechanical, diffable signal* that the checked-in client is (or isn't) in sync with the spec it was generated from.
- **Refit + Refitter**: "CLI/MSBuild regen from a checked-in spec; manual, low-ceremony." No lock file, no generated-vs-source drift signal of any kind.

The recommendation section is explicit that regeneration is "rerun `refitter` by hand whenever it changes, and commit the regenerated interface like any other source file." Combine that with the same section's own risk mitigation for Refitter's smaller/newer status — "its output is small enough to hand-maintain indefinitely if the generator stalls" — and you get a compounding problem, not two independent, separately-acceptable risks: the same property that makes Refitter's output cheap to *diverge from* on purpose (a plain, hand-editable C# interface) is exactly what makes an *accidental* divergence (forgot to regen, or hand-patched something and never reconciled with the next regen) indistinguishable from a deliberate one. Nothing in the design — no lock file, no committed hash of the source spec, no test — can tell "this interface was intentionally hand-maintained past where Refitter would generate it" apart from "this interface silently drifted stale against the real callee contract." That is precisely the failure mode the human owner's question 2 asks about, and the document's own chosen mitigation for question 2 (informal, manual regen) is what removes the one mechanism (Kiota's lock file) that would have made drift visible without needing full CI investment.

**Recommendation, not a reversal of the Refit pick**: either (a) adopt a lightweight, non-CI drift check — e.g. a short local script/make-target that reruns `refitter` against the checked-in spec and diffs against the committed interface, run manually before a PR touching either client, cheap enough not to need the CI investment requirement #4 explicitly wants to avoid — or (b) if that's judged not worth building yet, at minimum say so explicitly as a **known, accepted gap** (the same way the security note does for auth) rather than leaving "manual regen, low-ceremony" to read as if it's already an adequate answer to requirement #4. Today it reads as resolved; it's actually deferred.

### 2. "Not centrally mandated" — right conclusion, but the cited precedent doesn't fully support it

Checked the prompt's specific question: is there a real precedent for "genuinely local, not centrally decided" elsewhere in this project, and does it hold up the same way here?

**Yes, a real one exists, and it's a better analogy than the ones the document cites**: per-service persistence/ORM choice. ADR-005 mandates only that each service owns an *independent datastore* — it says nothing about EF Core vs. another ORM, SQL vs. NoSQL, or the actual DB technology. In practice `exercise-service` already runs a CQRS split (EF Core on the write side, a MongoDB read model — `exercise-service/architecture.md` → "Read model (MongoDB query side)…"), a genuinely local implementation choice never promoted to a central rule, and nothing in this project's docs suggests `training-planning-service` is expected to mirror it. That is a clean instance of "local decision, correctly never centralized" — because **the two services' datastores never interact with each other at all**. One service's ORM choice is invisible not just to the other service's build, but to the other service, full stop.

**The client-generation choice is not that shape.** It governs the tooling behind a call that crosses directly, bidirectionally, between the same two services, built (per the Architect's own framing) by the same small team. The document's own three cited precedents for "provisional default, not centrally resolved" — `DayOfWeek?` scheduling, hierarchy cross-visibility, current-version-only for "referenced" — are all *single-service, single-author* provisional defaults about **business/domain behavior**, decided once inside one service's own boundary. None of them involve two services' tooling choices needing to compose with each other, and none of them involve the same team making what is structurally *the identical choice twice* (once per direction) using *the identical requirements list* (ports-and-adapters fit, Polly composability, `IHttpClientFactory` idiom, regen story) both times.

This doesn't flip the conclusion — the document's stronger argument (no shared build artifact, no shared package reference, invisible to the callee) is correct and does the real work of justifying "not mandated." But the precedent-matching paragraph is doing weaker work than it looks like it's doing, and conflating "provisional domain-behavior default" with "shared cross-service tooling default" risks being cited as looser precedent than warranted the next time a genuinely two-party tooling question comes up (e.g. a shared contract-testing approach, or a shared logging/correlation-ID convention for these same two endpoints) — those are also "invisible to the callee's build" by the same argument, but consistency plainly matters more for them than it does here. **Recommend**: next revision either drop the three-precedent analogy or make explicit why cross-service-tooling defaults are treated the same as intra-service behavior defaults, since on inspection they aren't obviously the same kind of "local."

One more point in the document's favor that it doesn't make explicitly, and should: it already requires a service that deviates to record *why* in its own local decision log (the closing paragraph of "Central mandate or per-service default?"). That is a meaningfully stronger position than pure laissez-faire — it's "default-with-accountability," not "anything goes, unrecorded." Worth calling out as the actual mechanism doing the consistency-protection work, rather than leaning on the weaker precedent analogy to carry that weight.

### 3. ADR vs. non-binding addendum — the document-type call isn't licensed by the stated criteria it's supposed to follow

`docs/architecture/adr/README.md` states the inclusion test as: an ADR-worthy decision is one that is *irreversible or costly to reverse*, **or** *affects more than one service/feature*, **or** *touches security, deployment, system-wide architecture, or cross-service APIs/events* (three independent "or" branches). This decision:

- is cheap to reverse (both the document's own recommendation section and this review agree — Refitter's output is plain, hand-maintainable C#, and Refit itself is a thin `HttpClient` wrapper);
- **does affect more than one service** — by construction, it recommends a shared default for both `exercise-service`'s and `training-planning-service`'s client generation;
- arguably touches "cross-service APIs" tangentially (it's about the tooling for consuming ADR-006's cross-service APIs, not the APIs themselves).

Branch two is satisfied on a plain reading of the README's own text. The addendum's actual justification for staying out of `adr/` — "the recommendation itself concludes it should not be a binding cross-service rule" (`adr/README.md`'s own summary of it) — is a coherent argument, but it is a *new* criterion, invented for this case, that doesn't appear anywhere in `adr/README.md`'s stated rules. Nothing wrong with the outcome (recording a non-binding recommendation as a non-binding recommendation, not dressed up as a binding ADR, is honest and probably the more useful shape for future readers) — but right now this reads as *this document deciding its own document-type exemption*, not as an application of an existing, agreed rule. That's a precedent-setting move dressed as a routine one.

**Recommend**: either (a) add a short, explicit fourth carve-out to `adr/README.md`'s "What does NOT belong here" section — something like "non-binding shared recommendations/defaults that a service may deviate from without central re-approval, even if they'd otherwise qualify by the criteria above" — so the next gray-area case (there will be one) has a rule to point to instead of re-deriving this same reasoning informally each time; or (b) if that carve-out feels too broad to write down cleanly, treat this specific case as ADR-007 anyway, with "Status: Accepted (non-binding default)" — ADRs can record non-mandates just as well as mandates; nothing in the README's template requires a decision to *bind* anyone, only that it be *recorded* with rationale and alternatives. Either fix is cheap; leaving the current ungoverned self-exemption in place is the only option that quietly erodes the README's own stated criteria for future cases.

### 4. Does Refit-over-Kiota correctly weigh the Identity/`OwnerId` fan-out concern? — the premise itself needs a caveat this document doesn't carry forward

The human owner's question 4 asks whether today's one-method-per-direction shape is the wrong thing to optimize for, given fan-out is called a "near-certain next occurrence." Worth flagging explicitly: this iteration's **own earlier finding (§5, above)** already puts a load-bearing caveat on that exact premise — that "User/`OwnerId` fan-out" as framed by the Analyst/Architect likely conflates two structurally different problems (a probably-redundant-with-auth per-create existence check, vs. a real but low-frequency, cascading account-deletion reference check), and that the genuinely analogous "several narrow calls at once" shape may not actually be the one that materializes.

The client-generation addendum does correctly avoid over-committing to Kiota on the strength of that premise — "not the default today only because today's surface... doesn't yet need that scaling property" is appropriately hedged language, not a confident bet. But it also doesn't connect its own hedge back to the fact that the premise it's hedging against is itself disputed elsewhere in this same review cycle. As written, a future reader of just the client-generation section would reasonably take "Identity/`OwnerId` fan-out... near-certain next occurrence" at face value and conclude Kiota's forward-fit is a bigger looming cost than this review believes it actually is.

**Net assessment**: Refit-over-Kiota is, if anything, *more* defensible than the document's own stated reasoning suggests, precisely because the fan-out premise it's hedging against is weaker than presented — but that should be stated, not left as an unstated strengthening a future reader has to reconstruct by cross-referencing two separate documents. Recommend the next pass add a one-line cross-reference from the "Central mandate" or "Runner-up: Kiota" paragraph back to this review's §5 finding (or wherever the Analyst/Architect ultimately resolve the fan-out split), so the two arguments compound instead of sitting in separate silos.

Separately, checked and **not** a real risk worth adding: .NET version divergence between the two services. Client generation (Refit, Kiota, or otherwise) happens per-project, against that project's own target framework; a future .NET-version mismatch between `exercise-service` and `training-planning-service` would affect *many* things about running the two services together, but the *generator* choice on the calling side is not one of them — there's no shared build artifact or cross-project reference for a version mismatch to break. No change needed; noted so this doesn't get raised again as if it were new.

### 5. Security-gap framing — the flag is real, valuable, and under-scoped

Checked directly: no other file in `docs/` states, in so many words, that the system currently has no authentication anywhere. `identity-service/README.md` says only "Placeholder only... pending the deferred integration-pattern and technology-stack decisions" — it does not say "and therefore every service today accepts every request from anyone." The client-generation addendum's security note is, on inspection, the **first place in this project's documentation that says this explicitly**. That's a genuinely useful thing to have finally written down, and the note's own stated purpose — "so this silence is never later mistaken for 'already solved'" — is the right instinct.

But the framing undersells its own scope. The note's language throughout is about "**both ADR-006 endpoints**" and "these two existing call sites" — grammatically scoped to the two new endpoints this addendum introduces. Read in isolation (which is exactly how a future agent doing `identity-service`'s first central-loop pass will encounter it, per the note's own closing sentence pointing at that future reader), it reads as "there are two known unauthenticated endpoints," not "there is currently no authentication anywhere in the system, of which these two endpoints are simply the newest instance." The distinction matters for triage: a reader who takes the narrower framing at face value could reasonably conclude that patching auth onto these two specific call sites once `identity-service` exists closes the gap this note describes — when the actual gap (every controller action in both services today, per the same absence-of-auth-infrastructure this note itself confirms) is much larger and this note is the only place that says so.

This is sharper for **Rule 2 specifically**, as the prompt asks: Rule 2's endpoint (exercise-service asking "is this Exercise ID referenced") is read-only and low-sensitivity on its own (worst case of a stranger querying it directly is a minor information leak — whether a given Exercise ID is in use — not data corruption). The actually dangerous unauthenticated surface for the fail-closed direction is not this new query endpoint; it's `exercise-service`'s own pre-existing `Delete` endpoint (the human-facing one a user calls to trigger the whole flow), which — per the same zero-identity-service reality — is *also* unauthenticated today, for entirely separate, prior reasons this addendum didn't create and doesn't mention. The addendum's security note, by scoping itself to "the two ADR-006 endpoints," doesn't connect these two facts, even though they're the same underlying gap and the second one is materially higher-stakes than the first.

One more concrete gap in the framing: it never states the **network topology** these two endpoints run on — public ingress (same exposure as user-facing API surface) vs. a private/internal-only network path (service mesh, VPC-internal only, no public route). "Anyone who can reach the network path" is a very different risk depending on which of those is true, and the note doesn't say which one applies, or even flag it as unknown. That's a fair thing to leave unresolved (network topology may not exist as infrastructure decisions yet) but it should be named as an open unknown, not left implicit.

**Recommendation**: keep the note (it's valuable and correctly instinct-driven), but (a) reframe its opening sentence to state the system-wide fact first — "no service in this system authenticates any caller today; these two new endpoints are two more instances of that, not a new or separate gap" — before narrowing to the two specific endpoints, and (b) either state the network topology these endpoints run on, or explicitly flag it as undecided/unknown rather than silent. Also recommend the underlying system-wide fact (not just this addendum's narrow instance of it) get a pointer placed somewhere a future agent would find it without already knowing to look inside this specific client-generation subsection — `identity-service/open-questions.md` is a natural home, or a new cross-cutting security note at `docs/architecture/` scope, given every one of the four real/placeholder services shares this exact same gap today, not just these two.

### What holds up well (this addendum)

- The requirements list (ports-and-adapters fit, Polly composability, `IHttpClientFactory` idiom, workable regen story) is the right set of criteria and is applied consistently across all five options in the comparison table — no thumb on the scale for the eventual pick.
- Rejecting OpenAPI Generator for its Java-toolchain dependency, and NSwag for legacy-momentum-plus-monolithic-client-shape, are both well-supported, checkable claims (not hand-waved), and neither is disputed here.
- Explicitly naming "hand-written, no generator" as a legitimate alternative, not a strawman, and giving Refitter's marginal cost-over-hand-writing as the actual deciding factor, is honest reasoning — it would have been easy to skip that comparison and just assume a generator is obviously better.
- The "each edge gets its own explicit requirements a generator must satisfy" framing composes cleanly with this iteration's earlier recommendation (§3.2, above) for a maintained per-edge registry in `integration-patterns.md` — recommend the eventual registry table gain a "client-gen tool actually used, per service, per edge" column once services start building against this, which would also give the "same-team consistency" concern in §2 above a cheap, visible, non-mandated way to self-correct (drift becomes visible in one place, without needing a rule to forbid it).
